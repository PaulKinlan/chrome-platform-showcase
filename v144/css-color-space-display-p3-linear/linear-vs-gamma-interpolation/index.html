Linear vs gamma interpolation

Mix red and green light in the real world and you get a bright yellow. Mix them in srgb — on gamma-encoded numbers — and CSS gives you a muddy olive, because averaging encoded values is not averaging light. Linear-light spaces exist to fix exactly this, and display-p3-linear brings the fix to the wide P3 gamut.

The srgb / srgb-linear half of this page works in every current Chrome — those spaces shipped years ago, and they carry the whole argument. The display-p3 / display-p3-linear half needs Chrome 144; on older builds it declares itself unsupported below rather than faking a result.

The proof, live today: srgb vs srgb-linear

linear-gradient(to right in srgb, A, B) — gamma-encoded interpolation

linear-gradient(to right in srgb-linear, A, B) — linear-light interpolation

color-mix(in srgb, …) →
color-mix(in srgb-linear, …) →

Both readouts are real getComputedStyle() results, not page math: an offscreen element gets the color-mix() declaration and the browser reports what it computed — serialized in the mixing space itself, per §16.5. With pure red and pure green at 50%, both mixes read 0.5 0.5 0, but the numbers mean different things: in srgb a stored 0.5 is only ≈21% of the light (the chip re-encodes to about rgb(128 128 0)), while in srgb-linear the 0.5 is half the light, and painting it re-encodes to about rgb(188 188 0). That brightness dip in the top strip's middle is the artifact linear spaces eliminate.

The new pair: display-p3 vs display-p3-linear

CSS Color 4 §13.2 names display-p3-linear — with srgb-linear and CIE XYZ — as an interpolation space that is linear in light intensity, appropriate when "the result of physically mixing two colored lights is desired." Same endpoints as above, now in P3:

linear-gradient(to right in display-p3, A, B) — shipped in Chrome 111

color-mix(in display-p3-linear, …) →

Why the muddy middle happens

An sRGB or Display P3 channel value is gamma-encoded: 0.5 stored means roughly 0.21 of the light. Interpolating the stored numbers half-way between 1 and 0 gives 0.5 stored — about 21% light — instead of the physically correct 50%. Interpolating in a linear-light space does the average on light itself, then re-encodes for the display. That's the entire difference between the two strips; the endpoints are identical, only the path between them changes.

The same reasoning is why physically based rendering and HDR pipelines — Chromium's stated motivation for shipping this keyword — work in linear spaces end-to-end and only apply the transfer function at the very last step.
